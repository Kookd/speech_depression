Depression Symptom Prediction using Speech

Jacobson Lab - Spring Rotation

Author

David Kook

Published

May 26, 2026

Introduction

Motivation

  • Speech is a core component in how we interact with each other and our environment.
  • Speech can be collected passively, through smartphones, smart speakers, and even home appliances.
  • Speech is the result of complex biophysical interactions between the neurological, vocal, and respiratory systems.
    • Changes in any of these systems can lead to how we speak and sound.
  • Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common mental health illness with multifactorial etiology, which ranges from biological to socio-environmental causes.
    • Diagnosis of MDD and assessment of symptoms relies on subjective self-report, which is a source of information bias.
    • There is evidence that linguistic (e.g. emotional intensity) and acoustic features (e.g. pitch, speed) of speech have potential to be objective biomarkers of depression.
  • However, current research focuses on using speech to predict overall depression severity and not individual symptoms, which is more clinically informative and lays foundations for personalized treatments.

Goal

Use speech to predict individual depression symptoms.

Methods

NoteClick to expand

Data

  • The Tracking Depression Study
  • Audio-only and video diaries from ecological momentary assessments that were administered 3/day for 90 days
    • 9 symptoms assessed: anhedonia, depressed mood, sleep problems, fatigue, appetite changes, feelings of worthlessness, concentration problems, psychomotor changes, suicidality
  • Final analysis included 57 participants with 862 recordings used for analysis

Pre-processing

  • Determining sample size for modeling
  • Depression symptoms (labels) preprocessing
    • Originally on 0 - 100 slider scale
    • Dichotomized to 0 (symptom absent) and 1 (symptom present) using three threholds: 26827, 26837, and 26850
      • 50 = to create balanced classes
      • 27/37 = one of these were the average symptom severity in previous work that showed good separation between severe and non-severe symptoms
    • One symptom, trouble sleeping, was asked in the morning only so if a recording was from the afternoon or evening, the label would be missing not at random. To resolve these edge cases, I imputed their missing label in a two-step process:
      • If the subject submitted a morning EMA with all questions answered, I imputed the afternoon and evening EMAs with the same label as the morning EMA.
      • If the subject did not submit a morning EMA or if the morning EMA had missing values, I took the average of the closest available day before and after with available data to impute the label.
        • Why? Sleep problems are chronic and don’t fluctuate significantly throughout the day or between days. Sleep issues are not resolved overnight.
  • Speech preprocessing (CPU only)
    • Extract audio from video diaries and convert extracted audio and audio-only diaries to .wav format.
    • Resample audio to 16kHz and pad or trim audio to 30 seconds.
    • For later ablation study, denoise audio using Meta’s audio denoiser model (removes background and isolates speaker voice even with multiple speakers).
    • Extract speech embeddings using OpenAI’s base Whisper model to a .csv of n x 512 embeddings. Whisper is is a general-purpose model originally trained for speech recognition and transcription.
      • This model was selected based on the amount of data that it was trained on compared to other models like openSMILE.
    • Mean-pool the embeddings across the recording’s time dimension to get a single 512-dimensional vector representation of each audio.
  • Training and evaluation (GPU only)
    • Multi-layer feed-forward neural network with 2 hidden layers, ReLU activation, and dropout.
      • Cost function: BCEWithLogitLoss (combines sigmoid activation with binary cross-entropy loss to catch extreme values)
      • Adam optimizer for learning rate with linear warm-up and cosine annealing scheduler
      • Early stopping with patience set at 10 epochs to prevent overfitting
    • 5-fold nested cross validation split at the participant level to prevent data leakage using StratifiedGroupKFold.
      • Outer loop - 5 folds (~20% of participants), inner loop - 4 folds:
        • Within each fold, hyperparameters are validated and their scores averaged then ranked. Hyperparameter configuration with highest average score is tested on outer fold. Repeated for each outer fold.
        • Configs from inner fold then re-trained on outer fold train hold-out and validated on outer test hold-out.
        • Majority vote across 5 outer folds to obtain final model hyperparameters for later inference or deployment.
      • Hyperparameter grid search (8 combinations):
        • Hidden layers - [(128, 64), (256, 64)]
        • Dropout - [0.3, 0.5]
        • Learning rate - [1e-3, 3e-4]
        • Weight decay - [1e-4]
    • Trained for 100 epochs with warmup of 5 epochs
    • Model evaluation:
      • Primary metric: Symptom-specific AUROC
      • Secondary metrics: Symptom-specific F1, precision, and recall

Results Summary

  • AUROC for all symptoms are ~ 0.5 indicating that the models’ predictive ability is no better than chance.

  • Prediction is especially bad for suicidality. With F1 = 0, no signal from speech embeddings are reliably predictive of suicidal ideation.

  • Lower thresholds of 27/37 were modestly better and stable than threshold 50.

  • Loss curves show model is over-fitting to the data and memorizes participants rather than learning from the data.

  • De-noising audio did not improve predictions.

Next Steps

  • Using all embeddings instead of mean-pooling.

  • Re-aligning mismatched recording/EMAs to increase sample size.

  • Use alternative models besides Whisper to extract embeddings. For example, wav2vec is a speech-emotion recognition model, which might make it more appropriate in the mental health setting.

Demonstration

An HTML-based Streamlit web app was developed to demonstrate the model’s predictions on new audio recordings. Users can upload any audio file and choose which model to run (thresholds at 27, 37, or 50). Both raw and denoised audios are tested. Users can download the predicted probabilities as a .csv file.

Prior to running the app, create a conda environment in DartFS using the environment.yaml file. Copy over the app.py file and results/models/ directory to your DartFS. The results/models/ directory contains the final models that Streamlit uses for generating the app.

To run, use the following commands in DartFS:

  • cd speech_depression/
    • Or wherever you copied the app.py file and results/models/ directory in your DartFS
  • conda activate speech_depression
  • python -c "from denoiser import pretrained; pretrained.dns64()"
  • streamlit run app.py -- --models_dir results/models/
  • Take note of the local URL from Streamlit and the port number.

Then, open another terminal on your local device: - ssh -N -L 8501:localhost:8501 f0069vn@polaris.dartmouth.edu - Replace 8501 with the port number from the previous step if it is different. - Copy and paste the local URL from the previous step into your web browser to access the app. - Wait a few seconds for the app to load and then follow the instructions to get predictions on your audio file.
